Abstract

Human-Guided Local Robotic Polishing via Joint Motion–Force

   Representation and Force-Conditioned Imitation Learning

     Local surface rework requires coordinating tool motion, contact force, and time within a

selected region. This thesis connects human region specification and demonstrations to an executable

local robotic polishing skill. A joint motion–force representation temporally pairs Cartesian tool

pose with teaching-derived target force. Conditional Flow Matching generates action sequences

from RGB, pose, measured force, and force history. A shared executor conditions the references

for an existing force-feedback controller. Center-relative position learning and an explicit planar

direction adapter support reuse at registered placements.

     Three evaluations address strategy, force information, and spatial reuse. E1 compares rules,

force-inclusive demonstration replay (T), and the force-conditioned policy (C). Each strategy

completes the prescribed contact–loaded-pass–exit procedure in five observed runs. A separate

additional rule-retiming diagnostic (R∗ ) is kept distinct from the original rule cohort. Mean

completion times ± sample SD are 24.840±1.321, 12.897±0.041, and 13.420±2.630 s for R∗ /T/C.

T has lower time dispersion and excess-force exposure than C. Retiming reduces the rule mean by

4.697 s (15.9%) without matching measured mean speed.

     E2 separates pose-only learning with an external force target (A), motion–force learning with

masked force observations (B), and motion–force learning with measured-force conditioning (C). A

completes four of five attempts; B/C complete five each. Completed operator-based intervals are




                                                 vi
17.65±1.20, 18.81±0.40, and 15.83±0.56 s, with n = 4, 5, 5. C’s mean is 2.98 s (15.8%) shorter

than B’s. These descriptive intervals use a different completion protocol from E1.

     E3 reuses the same C checkpoint without new teaching or retraining. Original RGB reprocessing

with unchanged saved annotations, ROI, and gray threshold reproduces the board-marker ink-area

measurements. N/P1/D1/D2 contain 20 selected runs and 19 evaluable pairs. Removal means ±

sample SD are 100.00±0.00, 97.55±2.49, 98.12±3.75, and 93.67±6.60%, with n = 5, 5, 4, 5; final

tool occlusion remains unquantified.

     The results establish an executable human-guided skill, observed force-configuration differences,

and reuse with registration and direction adaptation in the tested planar placements. Rules and replay

remain effective fixed-task alternatives. Image-area reduction measures visible marker removal,

separately from procedural completion; metal removal depth, mass, roughness, and micro-scratch

restoration are not independently measured.

Keywords: Robotic Polishing, Human Guidance, Motion–Force Representation, Force-Conditioned

Imitation Learning, Spatial Skill Transfer

     Task specification identifies the local region and task setting. A motion–force skill is a

temporally organized sequence of pose and target-force references. Contact regulation is the

lower-level response to force feedback while executing those references. Procedural completion

denotes the specified contact–traversal–exit sequence. It does not denote verified scratch removal

or a roughness requirement. E3’s image-based removal is a separate ink-area metric. The letter C

names a condition within each experiment; E1, E2, and E3 executions are separate cohorts even

where the same checkpoint is reused.

Chapter 1


Introduction

1.1     Surface Rework and Local Polishing

      Surface finishing changes the condition of a component through controlled interaction between

a tool and its surface. Robotic polishing therefore involves more than reaching a geometric pose:

tool contact, feed motion, and processing duration jointly determine the operation. Research on

curved-surface polishing combines tool-path planning with force-controlled processing, while

coverage planning accounts for the contact region produced by the tool and workpiece geometry [1,2].

These studies establish an industrial and technical context for treating movement and contact as

coupled process decisions.

      Uniform processing of a whole surface and local rework around a selected region have different

spatial objectives. A coverage operation seeks to treat a prescribed surface systematically. A local

operation concentrates movement and loading around a limited target while retaining an approach

and an exit. In the latter case, the relevant questions include where contact begins, which part of

the region is traversed under load, how force changes during movement, and how the tool unloads

and rises afterward. A path that reaches both endpoints can still omit one of these procedural

requirements.

      Interactive surface-treatment systems provide a practical precedent for combining human

decisions, geometric planning, and robot execution [3]. The present thesis adopts a specific division

of work. A person indicates the local region, and demonstrations provide examples of how the




                                                 1
tool moves and loads that region. The robot executes a generated or prescribed motion–force skill

through an existing force-control system. This division is motivated by the equipment and task

setting, rather than a general assertion that fine defects cannot be detected automatically.

      The experimental surface contains visible markings that make the local task observable in

the available RGB stream. Such board-marker markings supply a spatial processing cue. E1/E2

measure contact-task execution, and E3 additionally measures image-based stain-area reduction

from saved before/after photographs. The markings are not calibrated models of metal scratch depth,

and erasing a mark is not equivalent to restoring a measured metal surface profile.



1.2     What Remains after a Region Is Specified

      An identified region is an incomplete processing instruction. It can supply an origin, extent,

and visual context without specifying a force schedule or the time spent in each phase. For example,

traversing a narrow region with a constant recipe force and replaying a recorded pass can share the

same region but differ in approach timing, loaded motion, repeated movement, and exit. The region

alone does not select between these strategies.

      Human guidance enters through a marked region and task registration. Position is learned

relative to the stain center; an implemented adapter aligns live XY and the policy image with

the teaching direction and restores generated paths afterward. This supports reuse across planar

placements and directions. The policy uses RGB and a fixed interaction ROI, with no external

segmentation mask. E1 fixes the origin, and both E1/E2 disable direction alignment. E3 evaluates

registered planar positions and directions, enabling alignment for its direction conditions.

      The second source of human guidance is a temporally paired demonstration. A demonstrator

supplies tool movement and interaction force together, so that approach, processing, and release have

a common temporal organization. This supplies information that a geometric boundary does not

encode. It does not provide labels for defect severity or an independent measurement of remaining

material. Learning from those demonstrations is intended to generate an executable skill, not to

infer an unobserved surface state by assumption.

1.3     Rules, Replay, and Learned Generation

      Several valid methods can turn a spatial instruction into a polishing sequence. Rules can

combine an approach, a prescribed path, a force recipe, and a departure. Replay can reuse the

position, orientation, force, and timing of a successful teaching episode. A learned policy can

instead generate a new sequence from the current image, pose, and force history. The existence of

several legitimate choices makes a comparison necessary: the insufficiency of geometry alone does

not imply that imitation learning is the only solution.

      The lower-level controller also contributes feedback in every method. It regulates the physical

interaction for the supplied target and operating mode. It does not independently choose the complete

spatial and temporal strategy. Conversely, removing measured force from the learned policy does

not remove the controller’s force feedback. Distinguishing these two uses of force is necessary to

interpret both baseline comparisons and ablations.

      Generative sequence policies offer a way to model temporally related actions from demonstra-

tion. Action chunking and diffusion-based policies motivate predicting a sequence rather than an

isolated next action [4, 5]. Here, conditional Flow Matching is the implemented generator. Its role is

to produce normalized pose–force sequences; coordinate restoration and command conditioning

remain explicit parts of execution. The thesis studies the combined representation and execution

structure without proposing Flow Matching, force sensing, or a recurrent encoder as new algorithms.



1.4     Research Questions

      Three questions organize the evaluation.

      RQ1: For the same local task, how do rule-based planning, force-inclusive demonstration replay,

             and force-conditioned imitation learning differ in procedural completion, completion

             time, and force use?

      RQ2: How do learning a target-force sequence and observing measured force at the policy

             input relate to completion, execution time, phase progression, and force use?




                                                  3
      RQ3: What image-based stain-area removal is observed when the same policy is applied,

            without new teaching or retraining, to task regions at different planar positions and

            directions?

E1, Strategy Comparison, addresses RQ1 with the original five nominal runs per strategy on 15

October 2026 and a separately identified five-run R retiming diagnostic on 19 October, denoted R∗ .

E2, Force-Information Ablation, addresses RQ2 with five attempts per condition on 13 October

1.5     Contributions

      The thesis makes three connected contributions at the system and experimental levels.

  1. It formulates a local polishing task that links human region specification with time-aligned

      motion–force demonstrations. It realizes stain-centered position learning and an optional

      direction-alignment/inverse-restoration path, distinguishes force signal roles and frames, and

      states the procedural scope of the resulting skill.

  2. It realizes observation-conditioned generation and execution of coupled pose–force sequences.

      The implemented policy, data construction, command conditioning, and existing contact

      controller form an explicit path from demonstration to robot action.

  3. It connects strategy execution, force-information composition, and spatial reuse through three

      complementary evaluations. These quantify procedural completion, protocol-specific timing,

      force use, and E3 image-based ink-area reduction. Original trials, incomplete attempts, and

      image-unavailable cases remain traceable, with additional diagnostics separated from the

      original comparisons.

Related Work

2.1     Robotic Polishing and Surface Coverage

      Surface-treatment planning and contact control address complementary aspects of a finishing

operation. Mohsin et al. [1] combine tool-path planning, force-controlled contact, and process-

parameter optimization for curved-surface polishing. Their evaluation includes an eyeglass-frame

application and surface-quality measurements. This supports the practical importance of considering

force and motion together, while also illustrating the additional measurements needed to make a

claim about the finished surface.

      Schneyer et al. [2] segment freeform geometries into regions with compatible machining

strategies. Their method uses tool and workpiece geometry, varies the contact point on a finishing

disk, and adapts path spacing to the contact area. The geometric region in that work organizes

coverage of a known surface. The local region in the present study instead supplies a human-selected

processing target, after which the choice of a temporal motion–force sequence remains.

      These approaches make geometric planning a substantive baseline rather than a weak alternative.

When geometry, material, and process requirements admit a stable recipe, explicit planning

can directly express the intended treatment. The narrower question considered here is whether

demonstrations and online observations add useful execution behavior in a specified local task. The

current experiments do not compare surface finish against the published systems under matched

tooling or materials.




                                                  6
2.2     Human Instruction and Local Task Specification

      RoboGrind [3] integrates surface scanning, defect identification, interactive robot-program

construction, planning, and force-controlled treatment. It also evaluates fiberglass wind-turbine-blade

refurbishment. Its user interaction helps select and parameterize processing, while geometric planning

and hybrid force–position control execute the resulting program. This is a useful example of human

participation within an automated surface-treatment system.

      The present work uses a simpler instruction channel: the person marks or specifies the local

region and provides demonstrations. Region registration is separated from learning how to traverse

and load it. This choice avoids requiring a fine-scratch detector in the available setup. It neither

replaces the broader perception problem nor demonstrates that a marked region carries sufficient

information for an optimal process strategy.

      A task-relative origin also has a limited but clear purpose. It removes an absolute XY offset

from the pose representation so that the local motion can be restored at a registered origin. An

image-to-plane homography provides a registration mechanism, whereas rotation canonicalization

is a separate transformation. Neither a task-origin subtraction nor a calibrated image mapping alone

demonstrates orientation generalization. The experiments must establish that claim independently.



2.3     Rules and Demonstration Replay

      Rules specify a finite program by composing a path, force schedule, and phase transitions.

Replay preserves an example’s motion–force association and timing without fitting a policy. In

a repetitive, narrowly specified task, these methods can be sufficient and easier to inspect than a

learned generator. Their adequacy is an empirical property of the task and execution environment.

      The distinction between replay and learning is particularly relevant here because the replay

baseline contains force as well as motion. A comparison against pose-only replay would mix the

value of retaining force demonstrations with the value of generating from observations. E1 instead

retains the selected demonstration’s force sequence. Both rules and replay also retain contact-start




                                                  7
synchronization and lower-level force feedback. They are therefore fixed strategy providers with

feedback during execution, rather than robots operating without contact sensing.



2.4     Force in Demonstrations, Observations, and Actions

      DexForce [6] makes a particularly relevant distinction between observed motion and an

action capable of producing the demonstrated force. It measures fingertip forces during kinesthetic

demonstrations and constructs force-informed position targets using a quasi-static force–displacement

relation. These targets are replayed through Cartesian impedance control to collect robot-only visual

demonstrations. Its diffusion policy observes images and, in the force-enabled condition, measured

forces and moments; it outputs force-informed positions rather than a separate desired-force vector.

The paper also ablates force observations. This motivates treating force in supervision and force in

observation as different design choices. Its dexterous-hand tasks and control interface differ from

the tool-pose and target-force interface studied here.

      ForceMimic [7] records motion and wrench with a handheld device. Its HybridIL method

conditions trajectory generation on point-cloud features and TCP pose, predicts pose and wrench

parameters, and executes them with position or orthogonal hybrid force–position primitives. The

described policy input does not list measured-force history. Thus, its force-inclusive action interface

should not automatically be interpreted as the same force-observation design used by condition C in

this thesis. Its reported task is vegetable peeling, rather than a human-specified polishing region.

      Admittance visuomotor policy learning [8] explicitly uses vision, proprioception, and contact

force to generate action trajectories and desired contact forces, followed by admittance execution.

FoAR [9] takes another route: force/torque information and a future-contact predictor guide reactive

refinement of position actions. It evaluates wiping, peeling, and chopping with an end-effector

position-control interface. These works illustrate that force can inform an observation, a learned

reference, or a deployment correction. Those roles should be compared directly rather than grouped

under a single force-aware label.

2.5     Observation-Conditioned Sequence Generation

      ACT [4] predicts action chunks to reduce the effective decision horizon in demonstration

learning. Diffusion Policy [5] models a conditional distribution over action sequences and executes

them with receding-horizon updates. Both provide methodological context for predicting temporally

related actions. They do not, by themselves, define a polishing force recipe or prove an advantage

over a suitable replay baseline.

      Flow Matching [10] trains a vector field along a prescribed probability path and generates

samples by numerical integration. The implementation in this project uses linear interpolation

between Gaussian noise and a normalized demonstrated action chunk. Its vector-field loss and

integration rule are specified in Chapter 5. Choosing this generator determines how sequences are

learned and sampled; it does not determine how physical contact is regulated.

      The image encoder uses the existing DINOv3 visual representation [11]. Global and fixed

interaction-region features condition the generator together with pose and force history. A pretrained

visual model is an implementation component, not evidence that the policy recognizes scratch depth

or remaining material. The present experiments evaluate the assembled policy rather than isolating

the backbone’s contribution.

virtual dynamics [13]. The deployed lower-level system is an existing element shared within the

3.5       Stain-Centered Learning and Direction Transfer

      The representation separates the local movement pattern from its placement on the plate. Its

training-data transformation expresses observation and action positions relative to a frozen stain

                         pek = [pk,x − cx , pk,y − cy , pk,z ]T ,   ρek = ρk .                    (3.2)

This transformation retains the base-axis orientation and absolute Z coordinate. A single-direction

demonstration consequently teaches a center-relative movement pattern in its original reference

direction. The implementation additionally supports transferring that learned pattern to another

in-plane stain direction through coordinated observation and action transformations. Training on

centered positions and aligning directions at inference are two connected parts of the transfer

mechanism; the dataset converter itself does not rotate the training episodes.


3.5.1     Center and Axis from the Specified Region

      The reference-free registration path detects dark pixels within a configured plate area, excludes

the tool area, and maps the retained pixels to robot-base XY coordinates using each image’s

pose-dependent planar homography. It pools these mapped points across the registration frames.

Starting from their coordinate-wise median, four iterations retain the nearest 85% of points and

update the center by their mean. A singular-value decomposition of the trimmed, centered point

cloud supplies the dominant strip axis. Its angle θlive ∈ [0, π) is measured in base-plane radians. The

axis is unoriented: the same strip has directions differing by π, rather than an identified stroke-start

endpoint.

live angle is also supported. The direction therefore enters through explicit task registration rather

than a learned output head. A center recovered from the visible mark identifies the intended task; it

does not estimate defect depth or determine the desired force schedule.

4.1     Teaching as a Source of Temporal Process Structure

local region. A demonstration records a tool’s approach, motion through the working region, and

departure together with the interaction force. This pairing supplies a temporal example for either

direct replay or policy supervision. It does not include an independently measured defect-depth

trajectory or a label that certifies material-removal completion.

and the first three force components. The paired data used by the evaluated models have a single

      The learning problem is to generate a future local skill from the execution context, while

preserving a joint representation of movement and target loading. A conditioned sequence can

change at a later inference call when the robot reaches a different pose or contact state. The policy

is not asked to estimate scratch depth, choose an abrasive, or output a separate completion decision.

5.2     Implemented Observation Encoder

      The active model is the Flow RGB policy in the archived training snapshot. It uses a frozen

DINOv3 ViT-small patch-16 image backbone, a state MLP, a one-layer force-history GRU, and a

fusion MLP. The separate encoder modules available elsewhere in the repository do not determine

this checkpoint’s architecture.

      The visual branch combines the global image feature with a feature pooled from a fixed TCP

interaction ROI. The ROI is constructed internally from reference image dimensions 424 × 240,

center (253, 120), and area-fraction parameter 0.25. The DINO patch features in that ROI are

pooled by the configured attention mechanism. Global and ROI features are each projected to 512

components. The ROI is a calibrated interaction-region prior, not an external defect mask and not a

prediction of surface damage.

      The state MLP encodes all nine normalized state components into 256 components. A GRU

with input dimension three and hidden dimension 64 encodes the force history, with its final hidden

state contributing to fusion. The fused condition is 256-dimensional. Thus the current force appears

both in the state vector and as the last history sample. There is no learned moment-history branch,

LSTM comparison, or termination head in the evaluated configuration.

6.1     From a Skill Sequence to Robot Commands

      Figure 6.1 summarizes the command-processing chain. The generated sequence is an upper-level

reference. Its physical meaning depends on the execution system that samples it, connects new

plans, gates force, and transmits commands to the existing controller. A common execution layer is

therefore necessary for interpreting differences between providers.

      The provider interface supplies a time array, pose–force values, a plan identifier, and whether

the plan is finite. R supplies a prescribed finite path with a recipe force. T supplies a finite teaching

sequence with its original time axis. C supplies successively generated chunks from observations.

Relative XY positions are restored using the frozen task origin before commands are interpreted in

robot-base position coordinates. In the optional direction-transfer path, inverse XY rotation precedes

that addition; the evaluated E1/E2 configuration uses translation alone. This restoration occurs

before downstream command conditioning. The accompanying force values remain controller

targets, with their frame interpreted by the lower-level configuration.

      The timed sampler uses elapsed reference time rather than selecting the closest spatial waypoint.

It linearly interpolates position and force and interpolates orientation on SO(3). This preserves




                                                   39
the association between a source time and its requested pose/force. It does not guarantee identical

physical feed velocity, because downstream conditioning and contact dynamics can alter motion.



6.2     Shared Pose Conditioning and Plan Connection

      The evaluated E1 configuration applies the same archived pose conditioner to R, T, and C.

Earlier metadata within the profile still refers to its initial C-only introduction; the active shared

6.5     Approach and Contact-Start Synchronization

      R and T retain an existing one-time contact-start synchronization. Before confirmed contact,

their approach reference continues to progress up to the prescribed processing boundary. Once

distinct received force observations sustain the existing ON state for 0.2 s, the reference clock is

aligned to that boundary. After the alignment, the finite strategy advances from its source timing;

repeated synchronization is not applied.

      This mechanism matters because the physical surface can be encountered before or after a

nominal geometric boundary. It supplies feedback to fixed strategies without learning a policy. C

instead generates its own approach and later chunks from observations. Common contact gating and

lower-level force regulation remain active in all methods.

      The normal departure is also provider dependent. R includes a planned zero-force upward

segment, T contains the selected teaching exit, and C can generate an exit within its pose–force

sequence. The evaluator recognizes the result of these actions. It does not insert a new retraction to

make a deficient sequence satisfy the task.

Discussion and Extensions

8.1     What the Implemented Connection Establishes

      The principal result is an implemented connection between a human-specified local task and

an executable motion–force skill. The teaching representation supplies pose, loading, and timing

together. The policy consumes observations and generates that same kind of sequence. The common

executor and existing contact controller make the output actionable on the robot. E1 examines

strategy execution, E2 separates target-force learning and force conditioning, and E3 measures

image-based ink-area reduction after spatial reuse.

      The primary E1 comparison establishes that this connection produces an executable local

contact skill. Rules, replay, and the learned provider each complete contact, the required loaded

traversal, and exit in all five planned runs. Their execution characteristics differ: T has a shorter mean

completion time and smaller time dispersion than C, and C has greater mean excess-force exposure.

E2 then shows a shorter operator-based completion interval for the force-observing configuration

than for B, together with an incomplete release transition in A4. E3 adds condition-mean ink-area

reductions of 93.67–100.00% in the evaluable registered placements. Together, the evaluations

support an executable skill, observed force-configuration differences, and spatial reuse with its

explicit adapter, while giving fixed strategies their observed value.

      The comparison also clarifies what an execution-level result can establish. A generated rise

can be learned as part of the demonstrated temporal structure. A gate can recognize unloading, and




                                                   60
an evaluator can confirm a displacement. None of those observations directly measures remaining

defect depth. Maintaining that distinction allows the method to be described positively and precisely

without assigning hidden process reasoning to the policy.



8.2     When Rules and Replay Are Sufficient

      The nominal task is narrow: a specified region, a single demonstrated direction, a fixed

setup, and an existing lower-level controller. Under those conditions, a prescribed strategy has a

strong opportunity to work well. T reuses a full force-inclusive episode and its timing, making

it a substantive alternative rather than a deliberately impoverished baseline. Its short completed

intervals and low observed dispersion indicate that a stable demonstration can be valuable without

policy learning.

      R illustrates the same point from a process-recipe perspective. A finite rule can express

approach, loading, traversal, and departure directly. Its longer interval reflects the constructed

strategy and contact synchronization, not an intrinsic limitation that all rules must be slower. The

executed additional R∗ diagnostic makes this sensitivity concrete: processing-only retiming is

associated with a 4.697 s (15.9%) shorter mean interval than original R. The dataset mean was

used as a quintic peak-feed parameter, not matched actual average speed. The separate date and

one-then-four collection history limit causal interpretation.

      Imitation learning is used here to represent the demonstrated temporal relationship between

motion and target force as an observation-conditioned generator. The specified region alone does

not determine that relationship, but rules and replay can also supply it. E1 consequently evaluates

learning alongside those alternatives rather than treating it as a necessary condition for completing

the fixed task. T’s strong nominal baseline and C’s executable sequence are compatible findings:

they characterize different ways to supply the local strategy.




                                                 61
8.3     Roles of Human Guidance and Relative Representation

      Human guidance resolves where the local operation is intended to occur. Teaching supplies an

example of how movement and loading can be organized. These are complementary contributions

of the operator. A spatial mask, mark, or origin cannot be treated as a complete polishing strategy,

and a demonstrated strategy cannot silently substitute for a new region specification.

      The relative representation supports this division by learning position around a stain center and

explicitly handling the stain direction at the policy boundary. A single-direction local movement

pattern is reused by aligning live XY and the policy image with the teaching direction, then

inversely rotating the generated XY path and adding the live center. This is an implemented

spatial-generalization mechanism, rather than a future proposal. It addresses placement and in-plane

direction through task registration while leaving observation-conditioned motion–force generation

to the learned policy.

      Its scope follows from the transformed channels. Z, tool orientation, and force are not rotated,

and image adaptation is an in-plane warp about the interaction ROI. Thus the mechanism transfers

the planar movement pattern without claiming complete pose–wrench equivariance or arbitrary-view

invariance. E1/E2 disable direction alignment; E3 uses it for D1/D2 and evaluates the registered

positions/directions in Table 7.6. Those observations characterize the combined policy and adapter

in the tested planar arrangement.

      The fixed image interaction ROI also has a specific function. It focuses part of the visual

representation around the calibrated tool interaction area while retaining a global feature. It is not

an explicit defect-state channel. The current experiments evaluate the resulting observation path as

a whole; they do not isolate whether a mark’s appearance, pose, or force carries most of the policy’s

decisions.



8.4     What Force Output and Observation Contribute

      Target-force learning allows the reference sequence to express a changing load, including

a departure from a constant recipe. Measured-force observation provides information about the




                                                  62
interaction that occurred rather than the load merely requested. Their roles are conceptually distinct

and are separated by the E2 contracts.

      The E2 observations identify differences in the organization of contact and release. C’s mean

operator-based completed interval is 2.98 s (15.8%) shorter than B’s, with five completed attempts in

each. This is a descriptive comparison of separately trained checkpoints, not the same weights tested

with a channel switched off. A4 exposes an incomplete transition in the motion-only condition, but

one interrupted attempt does not identify missing force output as the cause. The generated motion,

external constant-force interface, mechanical state, and timing can all contribute.

      The magnitude of force peaks also limits an uncomplicated advantage claim. C does not

reduce the E2 mean peak below A, and it has greater E1 excess exposure than R and T. A lower

whole-window force mean can follow from less time spent in contact. It is therefore inadequate to

call that lower mean an improvement in force-control accuracy or surface finishing.

      The force-frame convention is another practical boundary. The resolved teaching-to-base

relation supports empirical common-axis analysis, while the evaluated training file and online

adapter retain their actual numerical conventions. The force-conditioning path was not redefined

for this thesis. Its present performance is an observation about that implementation; a future

frame-consistent learning comparison would be a separate experiment. Similarly, the executor

suppresses tangential force commands, so a nine-dimensional learned action should not be described

as a physically demonstrated three-axis force-tracking capability.



8.5     Procedural Completion and Surface Quality

      The contact procedure is a meaningful intermediate target for local polishing. A tool must

establish contact, move through the relevant region under load, and leave the surface. E1 provides

explicit observations for that sequence. E2 contributes an operator-based interpretation of task

progression that preserves the interrupted attempt.

      E3 adds a directly observed processing outcome: the percentage reduction of segmented

board-marker ink area. N/P1/D1/D2 have means of 100.00, 97.55, 98.12, and 93.67% across 19

evaluable image pairs. These high condition means support practical reuse of the same policy with




                                                 63
task registration and direction alignment within the tested placements. The 83.879% D2-3 value

remains an individual observation, while D1-4 and P2 remain image-unavailable; the range of

means is not a minimum performance guarantee.

      A completed traversal, operator completion, and a reduced image stain are complementary

observations. E3 provides a quantitative ink-area outcome for its own spatial-transfer cohort. It cannot

establish E1/E2 removal quality or productivity at equal removal. Neither marker disappearance nor

an execution-derived heatmap independently measures metal depth, roughness, or micro-scratch

restoration. This distinction preserves the positive result while stating its physical scope.



8.6     Development Extensions

      Execution-derived dose heatmaps, finite blending paths, and adaptive reference-clock execution

are implemented development assets. The heatmap remains an uncalibrated diagnostic; the blending

and adaptive records do not provide a controlled E1–E3 surface-quality comparison. High-level

residual reinforcement learning for curved-surface restoration remains planned. Appendix E preserves

the diagnostic formulation, example heatmap, version distinctions, and implementation-status table.

These extensions are separate from the three evaluated contributions.



8.7     Scope and Limitations

      E1/E2 assess the nominal local task; E3 extends the evidence to the reported planar placements

and directions. Arbitrary shapes, curved surfaces, height changes, and reference-Z robustness remain

outside those observations. The tested direction transfer includes an explicit observation/image

adapter and XY inverse transformation. It is not an isolated test of policy equivariance or of force

observation’s causal contribution to spatial transfer. The inherited blending/RL assets retain their

separate implementation or planned status.

      There is one recorded training seed per learned condition, distinct from the repeated robot

executions. Original E1 shuffles R/T/C within five blocks, whereas R∗ is a separate-date one-then-four

diagnostic. E2 runs A, then B, then C, using separate checkpoints; A/B also changes action dimension




                                                  64
and force-target composition. Small cohorts and sample SDs establish neither statistical significance

nor equivalence or population reliability. Setup, execution order, and operator timing remain possible

contributors to the observed differences.

     E3 is a selected development collection with retained retries and dates. N uses the existing

HOME return, P1 uses PTP, and D1/D2 add commanded-release confirmation to PTP. These

differences preclude treating duration or path length as a controlled removal-efficiency ranking. The

image analysis is post hoc and semiautomatic, using stored photographs and saved visible-boundary

annotations. D1’s removal mean has n = 4; all five P2 final photographs and D1-4 are occluded.

Hidden stain area is not estimated. The common 40/45/50 threshold sensitivity check describes

segmentation dependence, not an uncertainty interval for future robot performance.

     The numerical evidence is traceable to frozen run identities and archived analysis inputs.

E1/E2 precision aggregates agree with their source CSVs; the additional R∗ retains unrounded

per-run values and template/configuration identifiers. E3 reprocessing from the original RGB, saved

annotations, and fixed ROI reproduces all 25 initial areas, 19 evaluable final areas, and 75 stored

mask/ROI arrays. This verifies computational reproduction of the established image analysis. It

does not independently validate its manual semantic labels or calibrate physical material removal.

     E1 automatic completion, E2 operator completion, and E3 image-area reduction remain distinct.

A4’s successful time is null; its 28.750 s interruption and all-attempt force/path values remain. C5

stays in the original C group; C6 is additional. Metal depth, mass, roughness, and micro-scratch

restoration are not independently measured, and an unrecorded controller-applied target still prevents

force-tracking-error claims.

Chapter 9


Conclusions

     Local robotic polishing requires a strategy for movement, contact loading, and time within a

selected region. Human region specification supplies the spatial task, but does not by itself determine

that temporal strategy. This thesis has addressed the resulting skill-generation problem through

paired motion–force teaching, observation-conditioned sequence learning, and a common robot

execution interface.

     Tool position is learned relative to a frozen stain center and paired with orientation and target

force in one sequence. The Flow Matching policy uses RGB, Cartesian state, and three-axis force

history. Its direction adapter aligns live XY and the policy image, then restores generated XY by

inverse rotation and center addition; Z, orientation, and force keep their conventions. E1/E2 disable

direction alignment; E3 reuses the same C checkpoint and normalizer for registered planar transfer.

The interfaces separate skill generation, contact control, and completion recognition.

     The first research question compares strategy providers for the fixed human-specified task. In

the primary E1 cohort, rules, force-inclusive replay, and the conditioned policy each complete the

required contact–loaded-pass–exit procedure in all five planned executions. Replay has a shorter

mean completion time and smaller time dispersion than C, while C has greater mean excess-force

exposure. The learned skill is therefore an executable strategy through the common robot interface,

and rules and replay are also effective alternatives within this nominal task. C5 remains in the original

C comparison and C6 is a separate diagnostic. The strategy summary distinguishes additional R∗

from original T/C. Processing retiming is associated with a 4.697 s (15.9%) shorter mean than




                                                   66
original R while preserving +18 N and the common execution settings. This separate diagnostic

uses a quintic peak-feed parameter and does not establish an IL advantage at matched measured

speed.

     The second research question compares the force-information configurations while retaining

lower-level feedback. The force-observing motion–force policy C has a 2.98 s (15.8%) shorter mean

operator-based completion interval than force-masked B, with five completed attempts in each.

Motion-only A completes four of five attempts; A4 does not progress to release and is interrupted.

It remains in all-attempt force, travel, and elapsed-interval statistics, while A’s successful-time

mean uses four completions. Timing, phase progression, and force exposure differ across the

configurations, without a monotonic improvement from A through B to C or an identified cause for

the single interrupted transition.

     The third research question evaluates spatial reuse through an image-based processing outcome.

In 20 selected N/P1/D1/D2 runs, 19 pairs permit whole-stain evaluation. Mean ± sample SD

board-marker area reductions are 100.00±0.00, 97.55±2.49, 98.12±3.75, and 93.67±6.60%,

respectively. D1-4 remains selected with an unavailable removal value due to tool occlusion. P2’s

five executed, occluded runs remain in the 25-run archive outside the main removal comparison.

The results support reuse of the same policy together with task registration and the direction adapter

within the tested planar placements, without new teaching or retraining.

     The demonstrated scope combines contact-skill execution, observed force-information dif-

ferences, and E3 image-based ink-area reduction. The latter neither establishes E1/E2 removal

quality nor independently measures metal surface restoration, roughness, or micro-scratch removal.

It also does not establish arbitrary geometry or height robustness. Existing dose, blending, and

adaptive-execution assets remain separate extensions with their documented status.

     Together, the three evaluations establish executable learned motion–force skills, observed

force-information differences, and registered planar reuse with image-area evidence. Rules and

replay retain their demonstrated value for the fixed task.

E.1     Execution-Derived Dose

      The repository includes an execution-derived heatmap recorder. It integrates a Preston-style

force–speed product along the measured path and optionally spreads it over a nominal tool kernel.

Its diagnostic quantity can be expressed as

                                    X
                        D(x, y) =        κ Fn,k vk ∆tk Ktool (x − xk , y − yk ),                (E.1)
                                     k


where (xk , yk ) is the recorded path location, Fn,k the implementation’s selected force component or

projection, vk its selected speed, ∆tk the observation duration, and Ktool a spatial spreading kernel.

Without a measured coefficient and tool-response calibration, κ defines relative scale and D has

arbitrary units. Different recorder/extension versions also make different kernel and speed-model

choices.

      Figure E.1 illustrates this uncalibrated diagnostic quantity.

      The implementation and generated artifact support calling this an implemented diagnostic

extension. They do not support the older claim of a calibrated material-removal model. Its spatial

distribution may help inspect where modeled processing exposure accumulated, but equal dose is

not demonstrated equal removal on the actual specimen.

   국소 표면 재작업에서는 지정된 영역 안에서 공구 동작, 접촉 힘, 작업 시간을 함께 구성

구현한다. 동작–힘 결합 표현은 Cartesian 공구 위치 · 자세와 교시 유래 목표 힘을 같은 시간축에

시퀀스를 생성한다. 공통 실행 계층과 기존 하위 힘제어기가 이를 로봇 접촉 작업으로 연결한다.

영역 중심 상대위치와 명시적인 평면 방향 어댑터로 등록 위치 · 방향에서 스킬을 재사용한다.
