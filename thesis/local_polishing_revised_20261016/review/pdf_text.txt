Abstract
  Human-Guided Local Robotic Polishing via Joint
 Motion–Force Representation and Force-Conditioned
                Imitation Learning

   Surface finishing often requires local rework around a selected region rather

than uniform treatment of an entire component. A local polishing operation

must coordinate tool movement, contact force, and processing time within

that region. In the setting considered here, a person specifies the region to be

treated, avoiding dependence on autonomous recognition of fine scratches with

the available equipment. This spatial instruction identifies where processing

should occur but leaves the motion–force sequence to be constructed. Rules

and demonstration replay provide legitimate solutions; the research question

is when observation-conditioned skill generation provides additional execution

value.

   This thesis develops a human-guided local polishing framework that con-

nects region specification with temporally paired motion–force demonstrations.

A tracked teaching system records tool pose, three-axis interaction force, and

RGB observations. Task-centered position learning is paired with an imple-

mented direction-alignment adapter that maps observations into the teaching

frame and restores generated XY paths. A conditional Flow Matching policy

generates sequences of position, orientation, and target force from images,

robot state, and measured-force history. A shared execution layer restores

task coordinates, connects plans, and conditions commands before an ex-

isting lower-level force controller regulates contact. Skill generation, contact

regulation, and procedural completion evaluation have distinct responsibilities.



                                      vii
   Two robot evaluations examine this division, with direction alignment dis-

abled. E1 compares rule-based planning, force-inclusive demonstration replay,

and the force-conditioned policy for a fixed nominal task. All five executions

per method complete the required contact–loaded-pass–exit procedure. Replay

has a shorter mean completion time and smaller time dispersion than the

learned policy, whose excess-force exposure is greater. E2 compares motion-

only learning and joint motion–force learning with and without measured-force

observation. The force-observing policy has a 15.8% shorter mean operator-

based completion interval than its force-masked counterpart in the recorded

cohort. One motion-only attempt remains in contact and is interrupted. These

results establish an executable, human-guided motion–force skill and reveal

differences in timing, phase progression, and force use across configurations.

Rules and replay remain effective alternatives for the fixed task. The eval-

uation concerns procedural execution rather than measured surface-quality

improvement or robustness across changed task conditions.


Keywords: Local Robotic Polishing, Human Task Specification, Motion–Force

Skill, Imitation Learning, Flow Matching, Contact-Task Evaluation

Chapter 1
Introduction

1.1      Surface Rework and Local Polishing

   Surface finishing changes the condition of a component through controlled

interaction between a tool and its surface. Robotic polishing therefore involves

more than reaching a geometric pose: tool contact, feed motion, and processing

duration jointly determine the operation. Research on curved-surface polishing

combines tool-path planning with force-controlled processing, while coverage

planning accounts for the contact region produced by the tool and workpiece

geometry [1, 2]. These studies establish an industrial and technical context for

treating movement and contact as coupled process decisions.

   Uniform processing of a whole surface and local rework around a selected

region have different spatial objectives. A coverage operation seeks to treat a

prescribed surface systematically. A local operation concentrates movement

and loading around a limited target while retaining an approach and an exit.

In the latter case, the relevant questions include where contact begins, which

part of the region is traversed under load, how force changes during movement,

and how the tool unloads and rises afterward. A path that reaches both

endpoints can still omit one of these procedural requirements.

   Interactive surface-treatment systems provide a practical precedent for

combining human decisions, geometric planning, and robot execution [3]. The

present thesis adopts a specific division of work. A person indicates the local

region, and demonstrations provide examples of how the tool moves and loads

that region. The robot executes a generated or prescribed motion–force skill


                                       1
through an existing force-control system. This division is motivated by the

equipment and task setting, rather than a general assertion that fine defects

cannot be detected automatically.

   The experimental surface contains visible markings that make the local

task observable in the available RGB stream. Such markings supply a spatial

processing cue and support qualitative inspection. They are not calibrated

models of metal scratch depth, and erasing a mark is not equivalent to restoring

a measured surface profile. The thesis uses local polishing as the application

and contact-task execution as the principal measured level of validation.


1.2      What Remains after a Region Is Specified

   An identified region is an incomplete processing instruction. It can supply

an origin, extent, and visual context without specifying a force schedule or

the time spent in each phase. For example, traversing a narrow region with a

constant recipe force and replaying a recorded pass can share the same region

but differ in approach timing, loaded motion, repeated movement, and exit.

The region alone does not select between these strategies.

   Human guidance enters through a marked region and task registration.

Position is learned relative to the stain center; an implemented adapter

aligns live XY and the policy image with the teaching direction and restores

generated paths afterward. This supports reuse across planar placements and

directions. The policy uses RGB and a fixed interaction ROI, with no external

segmentation mask. E1 fixes the origin, and both E1/E2 disable direction

alignment. The implemented transfer mechanism therefore has a broader scope

than these comparisons.

   The second source of human guidance is a temporally paired demonstration.

A demonstrator supplies tool movement and interaction force together, so that


                                       2
approach, processing, and release have a common temporal organization. This

supplies information that a geometric boundary does not encode. It does not

provide labels for defect severity or an independent measurement of remaining

material. Learning from those demonstrations is intended to generate an

executable skill, not to infer an unobserved surface state by assumption.


1.3      Rules, Replay, and Learned Generation

   Several valid methods can turn a spatial instruction into a polishing

sequence. Rules can combine an approach, a prescribed path, a force recipe,

and a departure. Replay can reuse the position, orientation, force, and timing

of a successful teaching episode. A learned policy can instead generate a new

sequence from the current image, pose, and force history. The existence of

several legitimate choices makes a comparison necessary: the insufficiency of

geometry alone does not imply that imitation learning is the only solution.

   The lower-level controller also contributes feedback in every method. It

regulates the physical interaction for the supplied target and operating mode.

It does not independently choose the complete spatial and temporal strat-

egy. Conversely, removing measured force from the learned policy does not

remove the controller’s force feedback. Distinguishing these two uses of force

is necessary to interpret both baseline comparisons and ablations.

   Generative sequence policies offer a way to model temporally related

actions from demonstration. Action chunking and diffusion-based policies

motivate predicting a sequence rather than an isolated next action [4, 5].

Here, conditional Flow Matching is the implemented generator. Its role is to

produce normalized pose–force sequences; coordinate restoration and command

conditioning remain explicit parts of execution. The thesis studies the combined

representation and execution structure without proposing Flow Matching,


                                       3
force sensing, or a recurrent encoder as new algorithms.


1.4     Research Questions

   Two questions organize the evaluation.

   RQ1: For a fixed human-specified local task, how do rule-based planning,

          force-inclusive demonstration replay, and force-conditioned imitation

          learning differ in procedural completion, execution time, and force

          exposure?

   RQ2: What differences in completion, execution timing, phase progression,

          and force exposure are observed across motion-only learning, joint

          motion–force learning without force observation, and joint motion–

          force learning with force observation?

E1 answers RQ1 using the original five nominal executions per strategy

recorded on 15 October 2026, with a common contact–loaded-pass–exit cri-

terion. E2 addresses RQ2 using the five attempts per condition recorded on

13 October. Both comparisons retain their common lower-level execution

environment. Height-change robustness and process-quality improvement are

outside this evaluation’s scope.


1.5     Contributions

   The thesis makes three connected contributions at the system and experi-

mental levels.

 1. It formulates a local polishing task that links human region specification

    with time-aligned motion–force demonstrations. It realizes stain-centered

    position learning and an optional direction-alignment/inverse-restoration



                                      4
    path, distinguishes force signal roles and frames, and states the procedural

    scope of the resulting skill.

 2. It realizes observation-conditioned generation and execution of coupled

    pose–force sequences. The implemented policy, data construction, com-

    mand conditioning, and existing contact controller form an explicit path

    from demonstration to robot action.

 3. It analyzes procedural completion, execution timing, phase progression,

    and force exposure through complementary strategy and force-information

    comparisons. The original planned trials and incomplete attempts are

    retained, and additional diagnostics are separated from the primary

    comparisons.

These contributions concern the connection between a particular task, repre-

sentation, generator, and execution interface. They do not assert novelty for

the individual encoder, relative-coordinate operation, controller, or logging

state machine.


1.6      Scope and Organization

   The implementation supports stain-centered learning and direction transfer

for the marked-surface task. E1/E2 use a fixed tool, fixture, and region, without

testing rotation transfer. Unseen curved surfaces and measured removal or

roughness improvement remain outside their scope. There is no autonomous

defect-completion detector or learned termination head.

   Chapters 2 and 3 position and formulate the task. Chapters 4 and 5 explain

teaching and learning; Chapter 6 describes execution and evaluation. Chapter 7

reports E1/E2, Chapter 8 discusses findings and extensions, and Chapter 9

concludes.

Chapter 2
Related Work

2.1        Robotic Polishing and Surface Coverage

   Surface-treatment planning and contact control address complementary

aspects of a finishing operation. Mohsin et al. [1] combine tool-path planning,

force-controlled contact, and process-parameter optimization for curved-surface

polishing. Their evaluation includes an eyeglass-frame application and surface-

quality measurements. This supports the practical importance of considering

force and motion together, while also illustrating the additional measurements

needed to make a claim about the finished surface.

   Schneyer et al. [2] segment freeform geometries into regions with compatible

machining strategies. Their method uses tool and workpiece geometry, varies

the contact point on a finishing disk, and adapts path spacing to the contact

area. The geometric region in that work organizes coverage of a known surface.

The local region in the present study instead supplies a human-selected

processing target, after which the choice of a temporal motion–force sequence

remains.

   These approaches make geometric planning a substantive baseline rather

than a weak alternative. When geometry, material, and process requirements

admit a stable recipe, explicit planning can directly express the intended

treatment. The narrower question considered here is whether demonstrations

and online observations add useful execution behavior in a specified local task.

The current experiments do not compare surface finish against the published

systems under matched tooling or materials.


                                       6
2.2      Human Instruction and Local Task Speci-
         fication

   RoboGrind [3] integrates surface scanning, defect identification, interactive

robot-program construction, planning, and force-controlled treatment. It also

evaluates fiberglass wind-turbine-blade refurbishment. Its user interaction

helps select and parameterize processing, while geometric planning and hybrid

force–position control execute the resulting program. This is a useful example

of human participation within an automated surface-treatment system.

   The present work uses a simpler instruction channel: the person marks or

specifies the local region and provides demonstrations. Region registration

is separated from learning how to traverse and load it. This choice avoids

requiring a fine-scratch detector in the available setup. It neither replaces the

broader perception problem nor demonstrates that a marked region carries

sufficient information for an optimal process strategy.

   A task-relative origin also has a limited but clear purpose. It removes an

absolute XY offset from the pose representation so that the local motion can

be restored at a registered origin. An image-to-plane homography provides

a registration mechanism, whereas rotation canonicalization is a separate

transformation. Neither a task-origin subtraction nor a calibrated image

mapping alone demonstrates orientation generalization. The experiments must

establish that claim independently.


2.3      Rules and Demonstration Replay

   Rules specify a finite program by composing a path, force schedule, and

phase transitions. Replay preserves an example’s motion–force association and

timing without fitting a policy. In a repetitive, narrowly specified task, these

methods can be sufficient and easier to inspect than a learned generator. Their


                                       7
adequacy is an empirical property of the task and execution environment.

   The distinction between replay and learning is particularly relevant here

because the replay baseline contains force as well as motion. A comparison

against pose-only replay would mix the value of retaining force demonstrations

with the value of generating from observations. E1 instead retains the selected

demonstration’s force sequence. Both rules and replay also retain contact-

start synchronization and lower-level force feedback. They are therefore fixed

strategy providers with feedback during execution, rather than robots operating

without contact sensing.


2.4     Force in Demonstrations, Observations,
        and Actions

   DexForce [6] makes a particularly relevant distinction between observed

motion and an action capable of producing the demonstrated force. It mea-

sures fingertip forces during kinesthetic demonstrations and constructs force-

informed position targets using a quasi-static force–displacement relation.

These targets are replayed through Cartesian impedance control to collect

robot-only visual demonstrations. Its diffusion policy observes images and, in

the force-enabled condition, measured forces and moments; it outputs force-

informed positions rather than a separate desired-force vector. The paper also

ablates force observations. This motivates treating force in supervision and

force in observation as different design choices. Its dexterous-hand tasks and

control interface differ from the tool-pose and target-force interface studied

here.

HybridIL method conditions trajectory generation on point-cloud features

and TCP pose, predicts pose and wrench parameters, and executes them with

can inform an observation, a learned reference, or a deployment correction.

Those roles should be compared directly rather than grouped under a single

force-aware label.

2.5     Observation-Conditioned Sequence Gen-
        eration

   ACT [4] predicts action chunks to reduce the effective decision horizon in

demonstration learning. Diffusion Policy [5] models a conditional distribution

over action sequences and executes them with receding-horizon updates. Both

provide methodological context for predicting temporally related actions. They

do not, by themselves, define a polishing force recipe or prove an advantage

over a suitable replay baseline.

   Flow Matching [10] trains a vector field along a prescribed probability path

and generates samples by numerical integration. The implementation in this

project uses linear interpolation between Gaussian noise and a normalized

demonstrated action chunk. Its vector-field loss and integration rule are

specified in Chapter 5. Choosing this generator determines how sequences are

learned and sampled; it does not determine how physical contact is regulated.

   The image encoder uses the existing DINOv3 visual representation [11].

Global and fixed interaction-region features condition the generator together

with pose and force history. A pretrained visual model is an implementation

component, not evidence that the policy recognizes scratch depth or remaining

material. The present experiments evaluate the assembled policy rather than

isolating the backbone’s contribution.


2.6     Contact Regulation and Positioning of

   Interaction control distinguishes the dynamic relation between motion and

force from the selection of a task strategy. Impedance control describes how

mechanical interaction should respond to motion [12]; admittance control uses

measured interaction to alter a motion reference through virtual dynamics [13].

3.5.1     Center and Axis from the Specified Region

   The reference-free registration path detects dark pixels within a configured

plate area, excludes the tool area, and maps the retained pixels to robot-

base XY coordinates using each image’s pose-dependent planar homography.

It pools these mapped points across the registration frames. Starting from

their coordinate-wise median, four iterations retain the nearest 85% of points

                                          17
and update the center by their mean. A singular-value decomposition of the

trimmed, centered point cloud supplies the dominant strip axis. Its angle

θlive ∈ [0, π) is measured in base-plane radians. The axis is unoriented: the

same strip has directions differing by π, rather than an identified stroke-start

endpoint.

   The resulting center and optional angle are frozen together for the episode.

An operator-supplied live angle is also supported. The direction therefore

enters through explicit task registration rather than a learned output head. A

center recovered from the visible mark identifies the intended task; it does not

estimate defect depth or determine the desired force schedule.

5.2      Implemented Observation Encoder

   The active model is the Flow RGB policy in the archived training snapshot.

It uses a frozen DINOv3 ViT-small patch-16 image backbone, a state MLP, a

one-layer force-history GRU, and a fusion MLP. The separate encoder mod-

ules available elsewhere in the repository do not determine this checkpoint’s

architecture.

   The visual branch combines the global image feature with a feature pooled

from a fixed TCP interaction ROI. The ROI is constructed internally from

reference image dimensions 424 × 240, center (253, 120), and area-fraction

parameter 0.25. The DINO patch features in that ROI are pooled by the

configured attention mechanism. Global and ROI features are each projected

to 512 components. The ROI is a calibrated interaction-region prior, not an

external defect mask and not a prediction of surface damage.

   The state MLP encodes all nine normalized state components into 256

6.1      From a Skill Sequence to Robot Com-
         mands

   The generated sequence is an upper-level reference. Its physical meaning

depends on the execution system that samples it, connects new plans, gates

force, and transmits commands to the existing controller. A common execution

layer is therefore necessary for interpreting differences between providers.

   The provider interface supplies a time array, pose–force values, a plan

identifier, and whether the plan is finite. R supplies a prescribed finite path

with a recipe force. T supplies a finite teaching sequence with its original time

axis. C supplies successively generated chunks from observations. Relative

XY positions are restored using the frozen task origin before commands

are interpreted in robot-base position coordinates. In the optional direction-

transfer path, inverse XY rotation precedes that addition; the evaluated

E1/E2 configuration uses translation alone. This restoration occurs before

downstream command conditioning. The accompanying force values remain

controller targets, with their frame interpreted by the lower-level configuration.

   The timed sampler uses elapsed reference time rather than selecting the

closest spatial waypoint. It linearly interpolates position and force and inter-

polates orientation on SO(3). This preserves the association between a source

time and its requested pose/force. It does not guarantee identical physical

feed velocity, because downstream conditioning and contact dynamics can

6.4      Existing Lower-Level Contact Regulation

   The archived controller receives a Cartesian pose reference and a signed

target-force vector. Its TCP-coordinate path rotates measured base vectors

into the local frame, performs contact-control computation there, and restores

the resulting motion to the robot frame. The stored controller configuration

identifies TCP force coordinates and an existing mass/damping adaptation

mode. This lower-level adaptation is a shared control asset; it is not the

upper-level residual surface-repair policy proposed in the old thesis.

   The underlying admittance implementation defines position error e =

6.5      Approach and Contact-Start Synchroniza-

prescribed processing boundary. Once distinct received force observations

timing; repeated synchronization is not applied.

strategies without learning a policy. C instead generates its own approach and

Chapter 8
Discussion and Extensions

8.1     What the Implemented Connection Estab-
        lishes

   The principal result is an implemented connection between a human-

specified local task and an executable motion–force skill. The teaching repre-

sentation supplies pose, loading, and timing together. The policy consumes

observations and generates that same kind of sequence. The common executor

and existing contact controller make the output actionable on the robot. E1

and E2 then examine the physical behavior rather than equating a valid

network output with successful processing.

   The primary E1 comparison establishes that this connection produces an

executable local contact skill. Rules, replay, and the learned provider each

complete contact, the required loaded traversal, and exit in all five planned

runs. Their execution characteristics differ: T has a shorter mean completion

time and smaller time dispersion than C, and C has greater mean excess-force

exposure. E2 then shows a shorter operator-based completion interval for

the force-observing configuration than for B, together with an incomplete

release transition in A4. The two comparisons support an implemented skill

and configuration-dependent behavior while giving the fixed alternatives their

observed value.

   The comparison also clarifies what an execution-level result can establish.

A generated rise can be learned as part of the demonstrated temporal structure.

A gate can recognize unloading, and an evaluator can confirm a displacement.


                                      64
None of those observations directly measures remaining defect depth. Main-

taining that distinction allows the method to be described positively and

precisely without assigning hidden process reasoning to the policy.


8.2      When Rules and Replay Are Sufficient

   The nominal task is narrow: a specified region, a single demonstrated

direction, a fixed setup, and an existing lower-level controller. Under those

conditions, a prescribed strategy has a strong opportunity to work well. T

reuses a full force-inclusive episode and its timing, making it a substantive al-

ternative rather than a deliberately impoverished baseline. Its short completed

intervals and low observed dispersion indicate that a stable demonstration

can be valuable without policy learning.

   R illustrates the same point from a process-recipe perspective. A finite rule

can express approach, loading, traversal, and departure directly. Its longer

interval reflects the constructed strategy and contact synchronization, not an

intrinsic limitation that all rules must be slower. Different recipe engineering

could change that tradeoff, but no unexecuted recipe is used to reinterpret

the measured comparison.

   Imitation learning is used here to represent the demonstrated temporal

relationship between motion and target force as an observation-conditioned

generator. The specified region alone does not determine that relationship,

but rules and replay can also supply it. E1 consequently evaluates learning

alongside those alternatives rather than treating it as a necessary condition

for completing the fixed task. T’s strong nominal baseline and C’s executable

sequence are compatible findings: they characterize different ways to supply

the local strategy.




                                       65
8.3      Roles of Human Guidance and Relative
         Representation

   Human guidance resolves where the local operation is intended to occur.

Teaching supplies an example of how movement and loading can be organized.

These are complementary contributions of the operator. A spatial mask,

mark, or origin cannot be treated as a complete polishing strategy, and a

demonstrated strategy cannot silently substitute for a new region specification.

   The relative representation supports this division by learning position

around a stain center and explicitly handling the stain direction at the policy

boundary. A single-direction local movement pattern is reused by aligning live

XY and the policy image with the teaching direction, then inversely rotating

the generated XY path and adding the live center. This is an implemented

spatial-generalization mechanism, rather than a future proposal. It addresses

placement and in-plane direction through task registration while leaving

observation-conditioned motion–force generation to the learned policy.

   Its scope follows from the transformed channels. Z, tool orientation, and

force are not rotated, and image adaptation is an in-plane warp about the

interaction ROI. Thus the mechanism transfers the planar movement pattern

without claiming complete pose–wrench equivariance or arbitrary-view invari-

ance. E1/E2 disable direction alignment and cannot establish its empirical

rotation-transfer range. The implementation’s existence and its unmeasured

performance within those comparisons are separate conclusions.

   The fixed image interaction ROI also has a specific function. It focuses

part of the visual representation around the calibrated tool interaction area

while retaining a global feature. It is not an explicit defect-state channel. The

current experiments evaluate the resulting observation path as a whole; they

do not isolate whether a mark’s appearance, pose, or force carries most of the


                                       66
policy’s decisions.


8.4      What Force Output and Observation Con-
         tribute

   Target-force learning allows the reference sequence to express a changing

load, including a departure from a constant recipe. Measured-force observation

provides information about the interaction that occurred rather than the load

merely requested. Their roles are conceptually distinct and are separated by

the E2 contracts.

   The E2 observations identify differences in the organization of contact

and release. C’s mean operator-based completed interval is 2.98 s (15.8%)

shorter than B’s, with five completed attempts in each. This is a descriptive

comparison of separately trained checkpoints, not the same weights tested

with a channel switched off. A4 exposes an incomplete transition in the motion-

only condition, but one interrupted attempt does not identify missing force

output as the cause. The generated motion, external constant-force interface,

mechanical state, and timing can all contribute.

   The magnitude of force peaks also limits an uncomplicated advantage

claim. C does not reduce the E2 mean peak below A, and it has greater E1

excess exposure than R and T. A lower whole-window force mean can follow

from less time spent in contact. It is therefore inadequate to call that lower

mean an improvement in force-control accuracy or surface finishing.

   The force-frame convention is another practical boundary. The resolved

teaching-to-base relation supports empirical common-axis analysis, while the

evaluated training file and online adapter retain their actual numerical conven-

tions. The force-conditioning path was not redefined for this thesis. Its present




                                       67
performance is an observation about that implementation; a future frame-

consistent learning comparison would be a separate experiment. Similarly,

the executor suppresses tangential force commands, so a nine-dimensional

learned action should not be described as a physically demonstrated three-axis

force-tracking capability.


8.5      Procedural Completion and Surface Qual-
         ity

   The contact procedure is a meaningful intermediate target for local polish-

ing. A tool must establish contact, move through the relevant region under

load, and leave the surface. E1 provides explicit observations for that se-

quence. E2 contributes an operator-based interpretation of task progression

that preserves the interrupted attempt.

   Surface quality adds a separate requirement. A completed traversal may

remove too little, too much, or unevenly depending on the material, tool, abra-

sive, speed, and pressure distribution. Visible marks may respond differently

from fine scratches in metal. Before/after snapshots with changing viewpoint

and occlusion cannot supply calibrated depth or roughness. Consequently, the

thesis’s measured results concern local contact-task execution, with visible

marking changes used qualitatively.

   This separation does not make the application incidental. It identifies the

part of a polishing workflow that the present system actually transfers: a

spatially registered temporal skill and its execution through contact feedback.

It also identifies what must be added before a process-quality claim becomes

defensible. The missing quality evidence cannot be replaced by a model-derived

heatmap or by lower force peaks alone.

tegrates a Preston-style force–speed product along the measured path and

optionally spreads it over a nominal tool kernel. Its diagnostic quantity can

be expressed as

8.8      Scope and Limitations

   The evaluation is confined to the fixed NOMINAL task. Physical height

changes, reference Z offsets, new positions or directions, new shapes, and

curved-surface performance are not evaluated. The direction-transfer adapter

and inherited extension assets retain their implemented status, but are not

experimental generalization results of E1/E2.

   There is one trained checkpoint and one recorded training seed per learned

condition, with five physical attempts per primary group. E1 shuffles methods

within its five recorded blocks; E2 collects A, then B, then C. Neither the small

groups nor the SDs establish statistical significance, equivalence, or population

reliability. Reported dispersion concerns run-level time, peak force, and travel,

not waveform reproduction or force-tracking accuracy. Setup preparation,

tooling changes, and operator familiarity can remain alternative explanations

for configuration differences.

   The completion protocols also differ. E1 recognizes the first specified contact

procedure using the recorded observer evidence. E2 includes the operator’s

observation and reaction, with possible lift and reapproach. Its 15.8% B–C

time difference therefore concerns that interval, not pure processing time or

productivity at equal removal. A4’s correction and C6’s separate diagnostic

role preserve the original observations without changing primary membership

or removing unfavorable cases.

   Surface depth, mass, roughness, and scratch-removal rate were not mea-

sured, so completion and visible marking changes support no quantitative

surface-restoration claim. Likewise, an unrecorded controller-applied force

target prevents a force-tracking-error claim. Future process-quality research

can build on this execution framework, with those additional quantities treated

as distinct scientific outcomes.

Chapter 9
Conclusions

   Local robotic polishing requires a strategy for movement, contact loading,

and time within a selected region. Human region specification supplies the

spatial task, but does not by itself determine that temporal strategy. This thesis

has addressed the resulting skill-generation problem through paired motion–

force teaching, observation-conditioned sequence learning, and a common

robot execution interface.

   Tool position is learned relative to a frozen stain center and paired with

orientation and target force on a shared sequence. The direction-transfer

adapter aligns live XY and the policy image with the teaching direction,

then restores generated XY by inverse rotation and center addition. Z, tool

orientation, and force keep their conventions. The evaluated Flow Matching

policy uses RGB, Cartesian state, and three-axis force history, with explicit

coordinate restoration, pose conditioning, plan connection, and existing contact

regulation. E1/E2 disable direction alignment and do not measure its transfer

performance. The implementation separates upper-level skill generation, lower-

level contact control, and completion recognition.

   The first research question compares strategy providers for the fixed human-

specified task. In the primary E1 cohort, rules, force-inclusive replay, and

the conditioned policy each complete the required contact–loaded-pass–exit

procedure in all five planned executions. Replay has a shorter mean completion

time and smaller time dispersion than C, while C has greater mean excess-force

exposure. The learned skill is therefore an executable strategy through the


                                       73
common robot interface, and rules and replay are also effective alternatives

within this nominal task. C5 remains in the primary comparison; the shorter,

lower-exposure C6 is a separate additional diagnostic.

   The second research question compares the force-information configurations

while retaining lower-level feedback. The force-observing motion–force policy

C has a 2.98 s (15.8%) shorter mean operator-based completion interval than

force-masked B, with five completed attempts in each. Motion-only A completes

four of five attempts; A4 does not progress to release and is interrupted. It

remains in all-attempt force, travel, and elapsed-interval statistics, while

A’s successful-time mean uses four completions. Timing, phase progression,

and force exposure differ across the configurations, without a monotonic

improvement from A through B to C or an identified cause for the single

interrupted transition.

   The demonstrated scope is the generation and execution of a human-

guided local contact skill on the marked-surface setup. It does not include

autonomous recognition of defect severity, learned recognition of complete

scratch removal, calibrated material-removal estimation, or measured surface

restoration. The existing dose, blending, and adaptive-execution assets provide

concrete extension paths, with their implementation and validation status

stated separately from the main findings.

   The thesis’s contribution is the coherent connection from local task speci-

fication to a time-related motion–force representation, conditioned generation,

shared execution, and corresponding evaluation. The fixed-task comparison

establishes actual execution alongside valid rule and replay strategies; the

force-information comparison establishes observed differences in completion,

operator-based timing, phase progression, and force use. These findings answer

the stated questions within the nominal contact-task scope.

A.1       Pose and Force Signal Definitions
   The position vector is expressed in millimeters and orientation as a ro-
tation vector in radians. The matrix R = Exp([ρ]× ) maps a vector in the
calibrated tool frame to the robot base when it represents RB←E . [ρ]× is the
skew-symmetric matrix associated with ρ. The saved dataset transformation
subtracts only (cx , cy ). The optional inference transformation additionally
rotates center-relative XY by R2 (α), with inverse rotation followed by center
addition on output (Section 3.5). Both retain Z, tool rotation vectors, and
force channels. The evaluated E1/E2 settings use α = 0.
   Teaching force is a three-component post-publisher measurement. Its asso-
ciated tool orientation supplies the base-axis rotation in Equation (4.3). Robot
wrench feedback is already base expressed. A torque reference-point transfor-
mation would require a moment arm, but no torque transfer is needed for the
three-force-component base projection used here. The available analysis does
not infer a calibrated compliant contact point from a TCP-center trajectory.

        모방학습을 활용한 국소 로봇 폴리싱

  표면 마무리 공정에서 부품 전체의 균일 처리와 지정된 영역 주변의 국소
재작업은 서로 다른 요구를 가진다. 국소 폴리싱에서는 대상 영역에 맞추어
공구 이동, 접촉 힘, 작업 시간을 함께 구성해야 한다. 본 연구는 현재 장비로
미세 스크래치를 자율 판별하는 대신 사람이 처리 영역을 지정하는 설정을 사
용한다. 공간 지시 이후에도 남는 동작 · 힘 · 시간 구성 문제에 대해, 규칙 기반
전략과 교시 재생에 비해 관측 조건부 학습이 어떤 수행 가치를 추가하는지
검토한다.
  추적 기반 교시 장치로 공구 위치 · 자세, 3축 힘, RGB 영상을 시간적으로
대응하여 기록한다. 얼룩 중심 상대위치를 학습하고 얼룩 방향을 학습 기준에
정렬한다. Flow Matching 정책은 영상, 로봇 상태, 측정 힘 이력으로 위치 · 자
세 · 목표 힘 시퀀스를 생성한다. 공통 실행 계층은 좌표 복원, 계획 연결, 명령
후처리를 수행하고 기존 하위 힘제어기가 접촉을 조절한다. 스킬 생성, 접촉
제어, 절차 완료 판정의 역할을 분리한다.
  E1/E2는 방향 정규화 없이 평가한다. E1은 고정된 기준조건에서 규칙 기
반 경로, 힘 포함 교시 재생, 힘 조건부 정책을 비교한다. 세 방법 모두 각 5
회에서 접촉–부하 통과–이탈 절차를 완료했다. 교시 재생은 학습 정책보다 평
균 완료시간과 시간 분산이 작았고, 학습 정책의 초과 힘 적분은 더 컸다. E2
는 동작 전용 학습과 힘 관측 유무에 따른 동작–힘 학습 구성을 비교한다. 힘
관측 정책은 힘 관측을 차단한 구성보다 평균 작업자 기준 완료시간이 15.8%
짧았다. 동작 전용 구성의 한 시행은 이탈로 전환하지 못해 작업자가 중단했다.
결과는 인간 지정 국소 작업에서 학습 스킬의 실행 가능성과 구성별 시간 · 단계
진행 · 힘 사용의 차이를 보여준다. 고정 작업에서는 규칙과 교시 재생도 유효한
대안이었다. 평가는 접촉 절차 수행에 한정되며 표면 품질 향상이나 조건 변화
강건성을 입증하지 않는다.
